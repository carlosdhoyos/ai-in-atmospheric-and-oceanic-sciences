Part 1 · The data foundation

Most of the work happens before the first neuron

A network only sees a table of numbers. Every choice that turns measurements into that table shapes what it can learn. Click a stage.

The rule of thumb. Garbage in, garbage out still holds. A bigger network does not fix a mislabeled target, a leaky feature or a test set that overlaps the training set.
Terminology. We say student attrition, not "school dropout", so it is never confused with dropout, the regularization technique of Part 2.

Measurement and acquisition

The same rain, three instruments, three different numbers

Data comes from platforms that measure different things. A rain gauge samples a point the size of a bucket. Radar infers rain from reflected microwaves over a few square kilometers. Satellites estimate it from cloud-top temperature or microwave emission over tens of kilometers.

  • Each source has its own resolution, bias and failure modes. Radar overestimates in the melting layer; satellites miss light rain and underestimate convective peaks.
  • Combining them is already a modelling decision: which one is the "truth" you train against?
  • The same happens outside climate: hospital records from different clinics, or enrollment systems that code a student's status differently.

Quality control and quality assurance (QA/QC)

Automatic checks catch most broken values before they reach the model

Quality assurance prevents errors (calibration, maintenance, clear protocols). Quality control detects them afterwards. Four classic checks:

  • Range: physically impossible values (−99 used as a missing code, a student aged 150).
  • Flatline: a sensor stuck at the same value for days.
  • Spike: a jump far larger than the series' usual day-to-day change.
  • Gaps: missing timestamps, which we treat on their own slide.
A flag is a question, not a verdict. A spike can be a real heatwave; QC decides what to inspect, and a person decides what to drop.

Encoding categorical variables

Pick the encoding that keeps the structure the category really has

  • One-hot: one column per category, a 1 in the matching column. No order is implied. Right for municipality.
  • Ordinal: one column with the rank. Right for estrato, whose levels have a natural order.
  • Embedding: a learned vector per category, inside the network (Part 2). Useful when there are hundreds of categories.

Transformations

Rescale so no variable dominates just because of its units

Scaling two features: income and age

Skewed variables: daily rainfall

Most days are dry or nearly dry and a few are extreme. A log transform spreads the small values and pulls in the long tail, so the extremes stop dominating the loss. Transaction amounts behave the same way.

Min–max maps to [0, 1] with the training minimum and maximum. Standardization subtracts the training mean and divides by the training standard deviation. Either way, fit the statistics on the training set only (see ).

Raw unstructured data into numbers

Text becomes token IDs; an image is already a grid of numbers

Tokenizing text

This toy tokenizer splits on words and punctuation and gives each new word the next free ID. Real tokenizers split rare words into sub-word pieces (Medellín → Med · ell · ín) so the vocabulary stays fixed, around 50,000 tokens.

An image is a grid of pixel values (draw on it)

8 × 8 = 64 numbers between 0 (empty) and 1 (full). A colour photo has three such grids (red, green, blue); a satellite scene can have dozens of bands.

Missing data · why is it missing?

The reason values are missing decides whether ignoring them biases the result

  • Missing completely at random (MCAR): a sensor battery fails on random days. Dropping those records loses data but adds no bias.
  • Missing at random (MAR): missingness depends on something you observe, e.g. rural schools answer the survey less often. Correct for it using that variable.
  • Missing not at random (MNAR): students at risk skip the survey because they are at risk. Missingness itself is informative.
Add a "was missing" flag as a feature. When missingness carries information (MNAR), the model can use it directly.

Survey of 400 students: average risk score

Missing data · filling the gap

Filling a gap in a rain gauge: neighbours beat the average

Options, from simplest to richest:

  • Drop the records with gaps.
  • Simple imputation: fill with the mean or median.
  • Interpolation between the last and next valid values; fine for smooth variables, poor for rain.
  • Model-based imputation: predict the gap from correlated neighbouring stations.

Whatever you choose, keep the flag: an imputed value is an estimate, and the model should know it.

Data splits

Three subsets with three jobs: fit, choose, and judge once

  • Training set: fits the weights.
  • Validation set: evaluated during development to tune hyperparameters and pick models.
  • Test set: untouched until the end; the final, unbiased estimate.
Terminology trap. Some fields (and some hydrology papers) swap "validation" and "test". Define them explicitly every time.

For time series and climate data, split by time: train on the past, test on the future. Neighbouring days are correlated, so a random split hides near-copies of test days in the training set.

Data leakage

If a feature only exists after the answer is known, it is leaking the answer

Leakage is information the model would not have at prediction time. Test scores look excellent; real-world performance collapses. Check for it every time you build features or splits.

Data leakage · the classic mistake

Fit the scaler on the training set only

Standardizing with the mean and standard deviation of the full dataset, before splitting, lets the test period's statistics into training. With a trend (warming, growing enrollment, inflation) the effect is large.

z = (x − μtrain) / σtrain
  1. Split first.
  2. Fit the scaler, imputer, encoder and feature selector on the training set.
  3. Apply the fitted transforms, unchanged, to validation and test.

A warming series: where is the split?

Dimensionality reduction and feature selection

Correlated inputs can be compressed into a few directions that carry most of the variance

Useful with many correlated features, few samples, or noisy high-dimensional data.

  • PCA / EOFs: new axes along the directions of largest variance. Climate example: a few EOFs of gridded sea surface temperature capture ENSO.
  • Filter methods: rank features by correlation or mutual information with the target.
  • Wrapper methods: try subsets and keep the one that validates best.
  • L1 regularization: pushes useless weights to exactly zero.

Health: a handful of informative lab tests out of thousands. Attrition: dozens of socioeconomic and academic variables reduced to the few that drive risk.

SST anomaly at two nearby grid cells: rotate the axis

Class imbalance

When the event is rare, 99.9% accuracy can mean the model never finds it

Fraud is maybe 0.1% of transactions; student attrition 5–15% per year; a rare disease far less. Predicting "never" is accurate and useless.

  • Metrics: precision, recall, F1 or PR-AUC instead of accuracy.
  • Resampling: oversample the rare class (or SMOTE), undersample the common one.
  • Class weights in the loss: errors on the rare class cost more.
  • Threshold: move the decision threshold to trade precision for recall.

A classifier's scores on 10,000 cases

Data augmentation

New training examples from changes that should not change the answer

Images: a radar echo (click to augment)

Medical images (X-rays) use the same tricks, but only those that keep the diagnosis: never flip left–right if the side matters.

Rare classes: SMOTE makes synthetic minority cases

Each synthetic point lies on the segment between a minority case and one of its minority neighbours. Text has its own versions: paraphrases, synonym replacement, back-translation.

Augment only the training set. Never the validation or test set, or you evaluate on copies of what you trained on.

Labeling and ground truth

A model cannot be more right than its labels

Who decides what counts as fraud, disease or attrition, and how consistently? Fraud is often confirmed weeks later; two doctors may disagree on a diagnosis; a student who transfers may be recorded as having left.

Noisy labels set a ceiling. Even a perfect model is "wrong" on the cases whose label is wrong, and it learns from those mistakes during training.

Delayed labels also matter: if fraud is confirmed 30 days later, the most recent month of training data is mislabeled as "not fraud".

Two doctors label the same 200 X-rays

Cohen's κ corrects the raw agreement for agreement expected by chance. Below about 0.6, the labels themselves are a research problem.

Outliers and anomalies

An outlier can be an error to remove or exactly the event you want to predict

A statistical rule flags values far from the rest. Whether to remove them is a domain decision.

  • A gauge reading of 900 mm in a day, next to dry neighbours, is a recording error.
  • A record 180 mm storm, confirmed by radar, is not noise. Removing it destroys what a flood model needs to learn.

Bias and representativeness

A model learns the population it was trained on, not the one it is used on

An attrition model trained only on urban schools may fail in rural ones, where distance, harvest seasons and transport change who leaves. The same applies to a rainfall model trained on lowland stations and used in the Andes.

Check the training sample against the deployment population, group by group, and report errors per group, not only the overall average.

Training sample: share of rural students

Risk rises with absences in both groups, but faster in rural schools. A single line fit to an urban-heavy sample underestimates rural risk.

Privacy and ethics

Removing names is not enough to anonymize a dataset

Health and student records are sensitive personal data. Colombia's personal data protection law (Ley 1581 de 2012) and Europe's GDPR require a legal basis, informed consent where applicable, purpose limitation and security.

  • Direct identifiers (name, ID number) must go.
  • Quasi-identifiers (age, municipality, school) can still single a person out when combined.
  • k-anonymity: every combination of quasi-identifiers is shared by at least k people.

Data versioning and lineage

Know which data, transformed how, trained which model

A reanalysis gets a new version and your model's skill changes. Without versioning you cannot tell whether the model, the code or the data changed.

  • Version raw data, code and configuration together (dataset version + git commit).
  • Record each transformation as a step with its inputs and outputs.
  • Store, with every trained model, the exact versions it came from.

Data governance

Governance ties quality, privacy, ethics, lineage and compliance together

The policies, roles and processes that decide how data is collected, owned, accessed, documented, protected and used. Models are only as trustworthy as the data and the rules around it.

Part 2 · Architecture

Every network is simple arithmetic, organized in four levels

The architecture is how numbers flow and connect: which values talk to which, with what weights, and what operation combines them. Multiply, add, apply a simple function, repeat many times. Click an item to see how it is built.

Unifying idea · inductive bias

Each operation hard-codes an assumption about who should be connected to whom

Seven days of input → the output for one day (click a day)

The geometric view

Choose the operation by the shape and symmetry of the data

Geometric deep learning (Bronstein et al., 2021): a good architecture respects the symmetry the problem already has.

A single neuron

A neuron multiplies, adds, and decides whether to pass the result on

y = f(w1x1 + w2x2 + b)

The worked example: x = [2, 3], w = [0.5, −1], b = 1. The sum is 2 × 0.5 + 3 × (−1) + 1 = −1, and ReLU(−1) = 0: the neuron stays silent. Change w2 to +1 and the sum is 5: it fires.

A dense (fully connected) layer

Many neurons side by side: a matrix multiplication plus a bias

y = f(W·x + b)

Each row of W is one neuron's weights over the same inputs. Three weather inputs to two outputs need 3 × 2 = 6 weights and 2 biases: 8 parameters.

Edit any weight in the matrix. Hover an output to see its row of the multiplication.

Inputs are standardized (z-scores), as Part 1 recommends.

Activation functions

A simple nonlinear function on each value

ReLU is the default in most hidden layers. Sigmoid gives a probability for a yes/no output. Tanh keeps values centred on zero (RNNs). GELU is a smooth ReLU used in transformers.

Why activations matter

Without a nonlinearity, ten layers are no better than one

Two dense layers without an activation are W₂(W₁x) = (W₂W₁)x: still one straight line. A nonlinearity between them lets each hidden neuron add a bend, and enough bends can follow any curve.

Target here: how runoff responds to rainfall. Nothing happens until the soil is saturated, then runoff rises steeply.

The best fit for each setting, found by least squares on the output layer with the hidden neurons' "hinges" spread over the range.

Softmax

Softmax turns any list of scores into probabilities that sum to 1

pi = ezi / Σj ezj

The worked example: scores [2, 1, 0] become about [0.67, 0.24, 0.09]. Bigger scores win more than proportionally, because of the exponential.

A temperature T divides the scores first: low T makes the largest score take nearly everything; high T flattens the probabilities. The same knob appears in attention and in text generation.

Season classifier: scores for tomorrow

Convolution in 1D

A small filter of shared weights slides along the series

Daily rainfall [0, 2, 8, 3, 0] and the filter [1, 1, 1] (a 3-day sum): 0 + 2 + 8 = 10, 2 + 8 + 3 = 13, 8 + 3 + 0 = 11. The filter [−1, 0, 1] instead detects increases. The network learns the filter values.

Dilated convolution skips positions: with dilation 2 a 3-tap filter covers days t, t−2 and t−4, seeing farther with the same 3 weights.

Convolution in 2D

The same idea on a grid: one 3 × 3 filter scans the whole field

On an image or a gridded climate field, the filter is a small square. At each position it multiplies the 9 values under it by its 9 weights and adds them up. The same 9 weights are used everywhere: that is weight sharing.

Precipitation field → feature map (hover or tap a cell)

Pooling

Pooling summarizes each neighbourhood with one number

Max pooling keeps the largest value; average pooling keeps the mean; global pooling summarizes the whole input. No weights to learn: it only shrinks and summarizes.

The 4 × 4 grid is daily maximum temperature (°C). 2 × 2 max pooling keeps the hottest cell of each block: [[25, 19], [15, 26]].

Downsampling and upsampling

Shrink to see context, grow back to draw a map

  • Downsampling: strided convolution or pooling halves the resolution.
  • Upsampling: interpolation, or a transposed convolution, which spreads each value over a larger output with learned weights. Used to turn a coarse 10 × 10 field into 20 × 20, as in downscaling.
  • U-Net does both: an encoder shrinks the input to capture context, a decoder grows it back to a pixel-level map, and skip connections pass fine detail across.

U-Net: the shape of the data through the network

Depthwise separable convolution

Split one big convolution into two cheap ones

A standard convolution mixes space and channels at once. A depthwise separable one first filters each channel on its own (depthwise), then mixes channels with a 1 × 1 convolution (pointwise). MobileNet uses it to run on phones.

standard: k·k·Cin·Cout    separable: k·k·Cin + Cin·Cout

Recurrence

Read one day at a time and carry a memory forward

ht = tanh(a · ht−1 + b · xt)

The worked example: a = 0.5, b = 1, h₀ = 0, standardized rainfall [1, 0, 2]. Day 1: tanh(1) = 0.76. Day 2: tanh(0.38) = 0.36. Day 3: tanh(0.18 + 2) = 0.97. The state remembers wet days and fades during dry ones.

The same a and b are reused at every step: an LSTM layer reading 30 days of streamflow reuses its weights 30 times.

LSTM and GRU: gated memory

Gates decide what to keep, what to forget and what to output

In a simple RNN the memory of a wet month decays by a factor a every day, so after a few weeks it is gone. An LSTM keeps a separate cell state c and a forget gate f between 0 and 1 that it learns to hold near 1 when the past matters.

ct = f · ct−1 + i · xt

A gate is a valve: a learned number between 0 and 1 that multiplies a signal. A gate of 0.1 lets 10% through. GRUs use the same idea with two gates instead of three.

Memory of a 10-day wet spell, 90 days later

Attention

Each word scores every other word, then takes a weighted average

  1. Score how related the chosen word is to every word (a dot product of learned vectors).
  2. Softmax turns the scores into weights that sum to 1.
  3. The output is the weighted average of the words' values.

For "it" in the cat sat because it was tired, the scores against [the, cat, sat, tired] are [0, 3, 1, 2], and softmax gives about [0.03, 0.64, 0.09, 0.24]: "it" takes its meaning mostly from "cat".

Multi-head attention in a forecast

Several heads, each learning a different kind of relation

In forecasting, today can attend to the most relevant past days, not only the most recent. Different heads specialize: one looks at the last few days, another at the same season last year.

Self-attention: elements of one sequence attend to each other. Cross-attention: one sequence attends to another, as when a forecast attends to satellite inputs.

Graph message passing

Each station updates itself from the messages of its neighbours

hi ← α · hi + (1 − α) · meanj ∈ N(i) hj

Real graph networks use learned weights on the messages; this keeps only the averaging. After one round each station knows its neighbours; after k rounds, stations k hops away.

Embedding lookup

A category ID picks a row from a table of learnable vectors

125 municipalities of Antioquia, each mapped to 4 numbers: a 125 × 4 table, 500 parameters. Municipality 17 → row 17 → [0.2, −1.1, 0.7, 0.0]. After training, municipalities with similar attrition patterns end up with similar vectors.

Normalization

Rescale intermediate values to keep training in a healthy range

Layer norm example: [2, 4, 6] has mean 4 and standard deviation 1.63, so it becomes [−1.22, 0, 1.22]. A learned scale and shift then adjust it: × 2 + 1 gives [−1.45, 1, 3.45].

Wiring tricks

Residuals, concatenation and gates: three ways to route the numbers

Residual (skip) connection

out = x + layer(x)

The layer only learns the correction. Even a layer that outputs 0 passes x through, which keeps very deep networks trainable.

Concatenation

Stack outputs side by side: satellite features next to station features before a dense layer, or encoder features next to decoder features in a U-Net.

Gating

A learned value between 0 and 1 multiplies the signal, like a valve. The core of LSTMs and GRUs.

Dropout (training only)

Switch off random neurons in training so no single one becomes a crutch

With rate 0.5, [4, 2, 6, 8] might become [8, 0, 12, 0]: the survivors are scaled up by 1 / (1 − rate) so the average stays the same. At prediction time dropout is off.

In a student attrition model, dropout of 0.3 after a dense layer of 64 neurons switches off about 19 random neurons at every training step, so the model cannot memorize one shortcut like "absences > 10".

Layers by position

The input layer is a shape, the hidden layers build representations, the task sets the output

  • Input layer: not a computation, just the shape of the data: 5 features per student, or a 64 × 64 field with 3 variables.
  • Hidden layers: early ones find simple patterns (edges, short rain bursts); deeper ones combine them (shapes, storm systems).
  • Output layer: chosen by the task. Pick one.

Common layer types · counting parameters

Count the weights by hand and "big models" stop being mysterious

Shapes and parameters through a network

Track the shape at every layer: the most practical skill there is

The worked example: a 28 × 28 image → convolution with 8 filters → 28 × 28 × 8 → max pooling → 14 × 14 × 8 → flatten → 1,568 → dense → 10 classes. Total 15,770 parameters, and 99% sit in the last dense layer.

Width and depth

Wider sees more patterns at once; deeper builds more abstract ones

Width is the number of neurons or filters in a layer; depth is the number of layers. An image model with three convolution layers finds edges, then corners and textures, then whole objects. A very wide but shallow dense layer can fit the training data yet generalizes worse than a moderately deep one.

Trainable or not?

Which layers learn weights, and which only move numbers around?

Dense, convolutional, recurrent, attention, embedding and normalization layers learn. Pooling, flatten and dropout do not: they summarize, rearrange or switch values off.

In the shapes example, the convolution has 80 parameters, pooling and flatten have 0, and the dense layer has 1,568 × 10 + 10 = 15,690. Total 15,770.

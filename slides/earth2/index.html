Physics vs emulator · why AI weather models are fast

A physics model solves equations at every small time step; an emulator learns one big step

  • Numerical weather prediction (NWP) integrates the equations of motion, thermodynamics and moisture on a grid. Stability limits the time step to minutes, and every step runs the physics (radiation, clouds, convection) on thousands of CPU cores.
  • An AI emulator is trained on decades of ERA5 reanalysis (ECMWF's best estimate of the past atmosphere) to map the state at time t to t + 6 h in one pass of a network on a GPU, then feeds its own output back in (autoregressive rollout).
  • The price: it can only be as good as its training data, it may drift over long runs, and it knows physics only through examples.

Step counts are illustrative (a 0.25° global NWP model typically uses a time step of a few minutes). 2 · Global forecasting · autoregressive rollout

A forecast is one 6-hour step fed back into itself: small errors grow

An AI global model learns a single map: the state at t → the state at t + 6 h. To reach day 10 it applies that map 40 times to its own output (autoregressive rollout). Here the "truth" is a toy 500 hPa field of drifting Rossby-like waves; the toy model gets each wave's speed slightly wrong, more so for the small waves, so the error compounds with every step.

FourCastNet (2022) · Adaptive Fourier Neural Operator

Mixing in Fourier space: every longitude talks to every other in one step

A vision transformer mixes its patches ("tokens") with attention, whose cost grows with the square of the number of tokens. AFNO instead takes an FFT across the tokens, multiplies each wavenumber by learned weights (a small MLP per mode), drops the high modes, and transforms back. Here: one latitude circle of the toy field, 128 longitudes.

SFNO (2023) · spherical Fourier neural operator

The Earth is not a flat rectangle: SFNO mixes in spherical harmonics

A 2-D FFT on the lat-lon grid treats the map as a flat sheet that wraps around top to bottom. But the top row is a single point (the pole), a grid cell at 80° is 6 times narrower than at the equator, and the north edge does not touch the south edge. SFNO replaces the FFT by the spherical harmonic transform: basis patterns Yℓm that live on the sphere, with no edges and no special poles.

compare a harmonic with a flat-FFT mode near the pole (drag the globe to look down on it); then move the round storm from 0° to 85° and read how many grid columns it covers.

Spherical harmonics are drawn as real parts Pℓm(sin φ) cos(mλ); a flat-FFT mode is cos(mλ)·cos(2πn·row/rows), periodic top to bottom. SFNO / FourCastNet v2: the lat-lon FFT "incorrectly assumes a flat geometry" (artefacts, dissipation); 73 channels, ERA5 0.25°, 6 h; stable rollouts for 1 year (1,460 steps), a year-long rollout in 12.8 min on one A6000; skill comparable to IFS to ~2 weeks (Bonev et al., ICML 2023, arXiv:2306.03838).

Deterministic models at long lead · MSE and blur

Trained on mean-squared error, a model learns to blur

At long lead the future is uncertain: many states are possible. A network trained to minimise the mean-squared error (MSE) is rewarded for predicting their average, and the average of many sharp, shifted waves is a smooth, weak wave. FourCastNet 3 (2025) · ensembles scored by CRPS

CRPS rewards an ensemble that is sharp and honest about its spread

CRPS = E|X − y| − ½ E|X − X′| = ∫ (F(x) − 1[x ≥ y])² dx

The continuous ranked probability score compares the ensemble's CDF F with the observation y. The first term pulls members towards y; the second rewards spread. On average, CRPS is lowest only when the members are drawn from the true distribution, so blurring or shrinking the ensemble does not pay.

drag the observation: an under-dispersed ensemble wins when y is near its centre and loses badly in the tails. Below right, find the spread with the lowest average CRPS.

FourCastNet 3 trains on point-wise CRPS plus a spectral CRPS (CRPS of the spherical-harmonic coefficients), so each member also keeps the right power at every scale. 16 members in training, 50 in evaluation.

3 · Data assimilation · why initial conditions matter

A forecast is only as good as its starting state, and errors in it grow

  • The atmosphere is chaotic: two states that differ by a tiny amount drift apart, slowly at first and then exponentially, until they are as different as two random states.
  • The toy is the Lorenz-63 system, three equations for convection. The same model runs twice; the second start differs from the first by the slider's amount in x.
  • The analysis is the best estimate of the current state, built from observations and a short forecast. Producing it is called data assimilation (DA). Every forecast, physics or AI, starts from one.

Classical analysis · optimal interpolation

The analysis weighs background and observations by their error variances

xa = xb + K (y − H xb),   K = B Hᵀ (H B Hᵀ + R)⁻¹

The background xb is a short forecast from the last analysis, y the observations, H maps the grid to the observation points (here: linear interpolation). B, R: background and observation error covariances. One observation on a grid point: K = σb² / (σb² + σo²), so trust whichever is less wrong. The correlation length L in B spreads each correction to the neighbours.

Observations · what the system actually sees

Observations are scattered in space and time; each instrument sees a different piece

  • Polar orbiters fly pole to pole and scan a strip below them; Earth turns underneath, so the gaps fill only over hours. They carry the microwave and infrared sounders that matter most for DA.
  • Geostationary satellites stay over one point on the equator and image the same disk again and again, but never see the poles.
  • Radiosondes: balloons launched at 00 and 12 UTC, mostly over Northern Hemisphere land. Aircraft, ships, buoys and GNSS radio occultation add more.
  • Satellites measure radiances, so classical DA needs an observation operator H (a radiative-transfer model) per instrument.

The HEALPix grid · equal-area pixels on the sphere

HEALPix: pixels of equal area, sitting on rings of latitude

  • HEALPix (Hierarchical Equal Area isoLatitude Pixelization): 12 base pixels, each split into Nside × Nside: 12 Nside² pixels. Doubling Nside splits every pixel into 4 (hierarchical).
  • Every pixel has the same area, so none is over-counted in a loss or a global mean. Lat-lon cells shrink to slivers at the poles.
  • Pixel centres lie on rings of constant latitude (fast spherical-harmonic transforms), and the 12 faces tile into square patches that ordinary convolutions can use.
  • Used by HealDA (v1 analysis on a 1° HEALPix grid), DLESyM (12 faces × 64 × 64 = 49,152 cells, ~110 km) and cBottle (HPX64 ~100 km → HPX1024 ~5 km).

5 · Nowcasting · the 0–6 hour problem

The next six hours: small, short-lived storms on the move

  • A thunderstorm cell is a few to tens of km across and lives tens of minutes to a few hours. A 25 km global grid cannot see it; you need a km-scale, convection-allowing model.
  • Nowcasting = forecasting the next 0–6 h. The classic baseline is extrapolation: estimate how radar echoes move and slide the latest image forward. Good for the first hour; it cannot grow, weaken or start a storm.
  • Km-scale NWP (e.g. NOAA's 3 km HRRR) simulates storms, but its first hours suffer from spin-up: it must build clouds and updrafts its initial state describes poorly.
  • Earth-2 learns the storm-scale evolution instead: StormCast from an NWP model's output, StormScope straight from satellite and radar.

5b · StormScope · inside the network · 2 · neighborhood attention

Each token looks only at its 31 × 31 neighbours; depth carries information further

Window

click a token in the middle, then one in a corner, and raise the layers: how many layers until one token can "see" the whole domain?

  • Self-attention: every token makes a query, compares it with the keys of other tokens, and takes a weighted mix of their values.
  • Global attention compares all pairs: N² = 28,672². Neighborhood attention (NA) compares each token only with a window around it: N · 31².
  • At the edges the window shifts inwards, so every token still sees 31 × 31 tokens.

Attention scores per layer and head

Paper §4.2.2: 2-D Neighborhood Attention with a 31 × 31 window "greatly reducing the compute required for self-attention while allowing long-range dependencies to be captured gradually over multiple layers"; 16 blocks; 128 × 224 tokens; one token = 4 × 4 pixels of 6 km = 24 km. NA and its edge behaviour: Hassani et al. (CVPR 2023, NeurIPS 2024) [paper refs 42–44]. Receptive field = our arithmetic: (31 − 1) × layers + 1 tokens, clipped to the domain. Background: toy IR image.

5b · StormScope · verification · 7 · fractions skill score

FSS: compare rain fractions in windows, so a near miss is not punished twice

Pixels above threshold

Threshold
FSS = 1 − Σ (Pf − Po)² / [ Σ Pf² + Σ Po² ]

Pf, Po: fraction of pixels above the threshold in an n × n window around each pixel, in the forecast and the observation. FSS = 1 is perfect; 0 means no overlap at that scale.

shift the forecast by 18 km with a 6 km window: pixel scores collapse. Now widen the window: from what size is the forecast "useful"?

FSS: Roberts & Lean 2008 (paper ref 32); "useful" when FSS ≥ 0.5 + f0/2, f0 = observed base rate (their criterion). The paper uses FSS with windows of 18 km at 20 dBZ and 42 km at 30 dBZ for nearcasts (§2.2.1, Fig. 6), 6/18/30 km at 40 dBZ for nowcasts (§2.2.2, Fig. 9), and 6 km at 235 K / 225 K for IR (Fig. 7). 6 · Climate · weather vs climate

Weather is one path; climate is the statistics of all paths

  • A single trajectory is unpredictable after about two weeks, but its statistics (mean, variance, extremes) are stable. A climate model must get those right, not the path.
  • An AI climate run is an autoregressive rollout of thousands to millions of steps. A tiny per-step amplification or bias compounds into drift: the model slides into its own wrong climate, or blows up.

Toy: x is a temperature anomaly (sd units) with ~2 weeks of memory, stepped every 6 h; the emulator learned the step with a small error:

xn+1 = 0.98 (1 + ε) xn + b + noise

start from "Stable": the paths differ after a few weeks but the histograms match. Then raise ε to 1 % or b to 0.005: when does the running mean leave the truth?

Histogram of steps 4,000–20,000

Toy model (AR(1) process), not a real emulator. Real numbers: SFNO ran stable autoregressive rollouts for 1 year (1,460 six-hour steps; Bonev et al., arXiv:2306.03838); DLESyM ran 1,000 years without drift (Cresswell-Clay et al., arXiv:2409.16247). At 6-h steps, 1,000 years is about 1.46 million steps, so an error that compounds per step must be essentially zero.

DLESyM · Deep Learning Earth System Model

DLESyM: a fast atmosphere and a slow ocean that talk every 4 days

  • Atmosphere network: 9 variables (geopotential at 5 levels, T2m, T850, column water vapour, outgoing long-wave radiation), 6-hourly states.
  • Ocean network: sea-surface temperature (SST) in 48-h steps. They are coupled every 4 days. Both on HEALPix: 12 × 64 × 64 = 49,152 cells, ~110 km.

Toy: a noisy wind u (memory 2 days) drives a slow SST with a recharge term. Two-way: warm SST changes the winds, which warm the SST further (a Bjerknes-like feedback).

cBottle · Climate in a Bottle

cBottle does not step forward: it draws a state for given conditions

stepping model: x(t + Δt) = f(x(t))
cBottle: x ~ p(x | SST, time of year, time of day)

A diffusion model (as in CorrDiff) samples a global state on HEALPix HPX64 (~100 km, 49,152 pixels) given the monthly SST and the solar position. No previous state, so nothing to drift. One sample is one possible weather; many samples give the climate.

switch from January to July and watch the warm belt and rain band move; set El Niño and look inside the box; press "New sample" a few times.

Compression. Trained on a km-scale ICON simulation (2020–2024) and ERA5 (1980–2017), cBottle squeezes petabytes of model output into a few GB of weights: ask it for any month, SST and hour.

cBottle: Brenowitz, Ge et al., arXiv:2505.06474 (generative diffusion; coarse generator on HPX64 conditioned on monthly SST and solar position; trained on ICON 2020–2024 and ERA5 1980–2017). The map is a toy planet (made-up continents, analytic seasonal and daily cycle, ENSO-like pattern, random weather), not cBottle output. Box: an ENSO region in the east-central tropical Pacific.

Huge ensembles · return periods, rare extremes

To see a 1-in-100-year event, you need hundreds of years of weather

A return period of T years means a chance of 1/T in any year. In N independent years the chance to see it at least once is 1 − (1 − 1/T)N. Observed records cover a few decades; a cheap emulator can run members × years of plausible weather.

Summary and references

  
Six ideas to take away

  

    

      
    
        
  1. Emulators learn one big step from decades of reanalysis and roll it out on a GPU, thousands of times cheaper than integrating the equations.
    2. 
        
  2. Geometry matters. SFNO uses spherical harmonics and HEALPix gives equal-area pixels, so the poles are not edges.
    3. 
        
  3. Mean vs samples. Training on MSE gives the blurry mean of possible futures; CRPS (FCN3) and diffusion (CorrDiff, StormCast, cBottle) give sharp, varied samples.
    4. 
        
  4. Every link is learnable: assimilation (HealDA), global forecast (FCN3, Atlas), downscaling (CorrDiff), nowcasting (StormScope), climate (DLESyM, cBottle).
    5. 
        
  5. Cheap ensembles change risk analysis: thousands of members make rare events countable.
    6. 
        
  6. Verify locally. Open weights and Earth2Studio make it possible to test these models on your own region and data.

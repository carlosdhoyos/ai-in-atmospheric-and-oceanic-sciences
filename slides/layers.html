Neural networks · Module 3

Layers: operations packaged with their own weights

A layer takes a tensor in and gives a tensor out. Operations (Module 2) are the mechanisms; layers are the units we stack. Here we count their weights, track their shapes and see why the bends between them matter.

Count

Dense 4 → 3 has 15 parameters. A 3 × 3 convolution with 16 filters has 160, whatever the image size.

Track

28 × 28 → conv → pool → flatten 1,568 → dense 10: 15,770 parameters, 99% in the last layer.

Bend

Two dense layers without an activation equal one dense layer.

Composite layers · LSTM and GRU

A whole small circuit of gates, used in practice as one layer

An LSTM layer is several dense operations, sigmoid gates and a multiply–add memory, repeated at every time step with the same weights. A GRU merges the gates into two and drops the separate cell. One unit here; move the inputs and watch each gate.

Why activations matter between layers

Without a nonlinearity, ten layers are no better than one

Two dense layers without an activation are W₂(W₁x) = (W₂W₁)x: still one straight line. A nonlinearity between them lets each hidden neuron add a bend, and enough bends can follow any curve.

Target: how runoff responds to rainfall. Nothing happens until the soil is saturated, then runoff rises steeply.

The best fit for each setting, found by least squares on the output layer with the hidden neurons' bends spread over the range.

Width and depth

Wider sees more patterns at once; deeper builds more abstract ones

Width is the number of neurons or filters in a layer; depth is the number of layers. An image model with three convolution layers finds edges, then corners and textures, then whole objects. A very wide but shallow network can fit the training data yet often generalizes worse than a moderately deep one.

Shapes and parameters through a network

Track the shape at every layer: the most practical skill there is

The worked example: a 28 × 28 image → convolution with 8 filters → 28 × 28 × 8 → max pooling → 14 × 14 × 8 → flatten → 1,568 → dense → 10 classes. Total 15,770 parameters, and 99% sit in the last dense layer.

Trainable or not?

Which layers learn weights, and which only move numbers around?

Dense, convolutional, recurrent, attention, embedding and normalization layers learn. Pooling, flatten and dropout do not: they summarize, rearrange or switch values off.

In the shapes example, the convolution has 80 parameters, pooling and flatten have 0, and the dense layer has 1,568 × 10 + 10 = 15,690. Total 15,770.

Batch norm also keeps running means and variances. They are stored with the model but not trained by gradient descent: "non-trainable parameters" in most libraries.

Summary

Layers own the weights; count them, track the shapes, and bend between them

What to remember

  • A layer = operations + their weights, tensor in → tensor out.
  • Input is a shape; hidden layers build representations; the task shapes the output.
  • Parameters: dense in × out + out; conv k·k·c_in·f + f; pooling, flatten and dropout 0.
  • LSTM and GRU are composite layers: gates and memory, the same weights at every step.
  • Without activations, stacked layers collapse into one.
